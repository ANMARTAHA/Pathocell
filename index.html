Acute Inflammation — the definitions, signs, sequences and mechanisms condensed for review.

Overview

The essentials only — definitions and the key facts worth memorizing, not the full lecture.

Definition

The local response of living mammalian tissue to injury from any agent. A defense reaction that eliminates the injurious agent and removes necrosed tissue — but it can itself damage tissue.

Cardinal signs

  • Redness — hyperaemia
  • Heat — hyperaemia
  • Swelling — exudation
  • Pain — nerve stimulation, chemical mediators
  • Loss of function

Three types

  • Acute: rapid onset, severe, lasts days–weeks
  • Subacute: intermediate between acute and chronic
  • Chronic: failed acute resolution, autoimmune, or a persistent low-grade irritant; inflammation + repair occur together

Causes

Exogenous: physical (mechanical, thermal), chemical (gases, acids, bases), biological (bacteria, viruses, parasites).

Endogenous: circulation disorders (thrombosis, infarction, hemorrhage), enzyme activation (acute pancreatitis), metabolic product deposits (uric acid, urea).

Pathogenesis — three processes

1 · Increased blood flow
→ redness and warmth
2 · Increased permeability
→ swelling / edema
3 · Leukocytic infiltration
→ cellular defense at the site

Hemodynamic sequence

Transient vasoconstriction
Seconds; lasts up to ~5 min in severe injury
Vasodilatation + stasis
Obvious within 30 min; "active hyperemia" → redness/warmth
Increased vessel permeability
Protein-rich fluid leaves vessels → exudate
Fluid exudate / edema
Raised hydrostatic pressure → swelling

Transudate vs. exudate

Transudate: low protein, hydrostatic/oncotic pressure problem.

Exudate: high protein (incl. immunoglobulins), caused by increased vascular permeability.

Leukocyte journey (order matters)

  • Margination & pavementing
  • Rolling & adhesion
  • Emigration & diapedesis
  • Chemotaxis

Phagocytosis — 3 steps

  • Recognition & attachment (mannose/scavenger receptors; opsonins: IgG, C3b, lectins)
  • Engulfment (pseudopods, actin, phagocytic vacuole)
  • Killing & degradation (O₂-dependent or -independent)

Killing mechanisms

O₂-dependent: myeloperoxidase-H₂O₂-halide → HOCl (bleach); or superoxide/hydroxyl radicals via glutathione peroxidase.

O₂-independent: lysosome, lactoferrin.

Chemical mediators — quick reference

Histamine
Mast cells / basophils → vascular leakage
Prostaglandins
All leukocytes → vasodilation, pain, fever
Lysosomal enzymes
Neutrophils / macrophages → bacterial & tissue destruction
Cytokines
Macrophages / lymphocytes → leukocyte activation
Leukotrienes (LTB4)
All leukocytes → chemoattractant
Nitric oxide
Macrophages → leukocyte activation
Complement C3a/C5a
Vascular permeability, chemotaxis (C5a), opsonization (C3b)
Factor XII (Hageman)
Activates kinin, clotting, fibrinolytic & complement systems

Systemic manifestations

  • Pyrexia (fever) — prostaglandin effect
  • Negative nitrogen balance
  • ↑ ESR, acute-phase proteins (CRP, SAA, SAP)
  • Leukocytosis (bacterial) / Leucopenia (typhoid, viral, rickettsial, protozoal)
  • Acute-phase reactions: malaise, hypotension, ↑pulse, rigors, chills

Morphological types

  • Catarrhal — mild, mucous membranes, excess mucus
  • Serous — clear watery effusion in serous cavities
  • Suppurative — pus, pyogenic bacteria, abscess/empyema
  • Fibrinous — fibrin deposits, "bread and butter" appearance
  • Pseudomembranous — necrosis + pseudomembrane (diphtheria)
  • Gangrenous — necrosis + putrefaction, foul odor
  • Ulcerative — loss of surface epithelium continuity

Outcomes

  • Resolution
  • Healing by repair or regeneration
  • Progression to chronic inflammation
  • Spread — direct (cellulitis), lymphatic, blood (septicemia)
  • Death
